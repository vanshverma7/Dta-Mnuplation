Worth unpacking before you reply, because how you interpret it shapes what work the requester is actually asking for.
Literally, veracity = truthfulness, accuracy, conformity to reality. Applied to recorded data values, it usually means one or more of these:

Are the values accurate — do they match what actually happened? (e.g. if a member requested £5,000, is £5,000 what's recorded?)
Are the values internally consistent — do related fields agree with each other? (e.g. if status = Approved, should the actual amount really be £0?)
Are the values plausible — within a sensible range, no impossible entries? (e.g. £88m requested, negative amounts, zeros where there shouldn't be any)
Are the values consistent across sources — does the same transfer carry the same value in different systems/extracts? (this is the main-vs-D&I check we already did)

In the data world there's also the well-known "5 Vs of big data" framing — Volume, Velocity, Variety, Veracity, Value — where veracity specifically means trustworthiness / data quality / how much you can rely on it for decisions. That's almost certainly the sense the requester is using, because it's the standard term in data-governance and analytics circles. It's broader than "accurate" — it covers completeness, consistency, plausibility, and reliability all together.
So when they say "look into the veracity of the recorded values", they're most likely asking: how much can we trust these amount fields? Are they good enough to use for analysis/reporting/decisions? — not narrowly "are these the literally correct numbers" (which often can't be answered without going back to source systems).
That framing actually fits well with what we've already done:

The main-vs-D&I comparison tests cross-source consistency
The £0 approved transfers and outlier checks test plausibility
The requested-vs-actual gaps test internal consistency
The missing requested-amount field on Transfers Out is a completeness gap

All four are facets of veracity in that broader sense, which is why the findings table works as a response — you've covered the question from multiple angles rather than narrowly.
